## On the Non-Reproducibility of Latency Benchmarks Across Deployment Environments

Published latency benchmarks for distributed databases are typically read as portable measures of performance, figures a reader can compare across vendors and deployment contexts as though the context were incidental to the result. This paper argues that it is not: the environment in which a benchmark runs is constitutive of the number it produces, and the conventions for reporting such benchmarks strip out precisely the variables that determine whether a figure generalizes elsewhere, an effect visible first in hardware and network topology, more sharply in the database's own configuration space, and, beneath both, in the workload used to produce the number. Node placement relative to availability zones and the degree of resource contention from co-located workloads are rarely disclosed at a granularity fine enough to replicate, though each moves the reported number on its own. Because the configuration space of a distributed database is large, one reported configuration, however carefully tuned, says little about behavior under a different consistency level or replication factor, and the party running the benchmark has every incentive to report the configuration that flatters the system rather than the one an operator will run. Underspecified or synthetic more often than not, the workload behind a reported figure, its key skew and read/write ratio in particular, is frequently decoupled from the traffic the receiving system will actually see. That gap is a structural feature of how these benchmarks are produced, not an incidental threat to external validity correctable by a footnote about scope: the measuring party and the party who will act on the measurement rarely share an incentive to make the number transferable. The paper closes by specifying what a reproducible latency benchmark would need to disclose about environment, configuration, and, most easily overlooked, workload, and by arguing that citing point-estimate latency figures without that context is a methodological defect in the field rather than an accepted convention.
