## Title
Why Published Latency Benchmarks for Distributed Databases Fail to Reproduce Across Deployment Environments

Latency figures reported for distributed database systems are routinely used to compare products, justify architectural decisions, and set service-level expectations, yet practitioners who attempt to reproduce these figures in their own environments frequently obtain results that diverge substantially from the published numbers. This paper argues that such divergence is not primarily attributable to measurement error or vendor misrepresentation, but is instead a structural consequence of how latency benchmarks are constructed and reported. We identify several sources of environment sensitivity that published benchmarks typically fail to control for or disclose: network topology and inter-node distance, the underlying virtualization and storage substrate, background noise from co-tenant workloads in shared infrastructure, client-side driver configuration, and the specific consistency and replication settings in effect during the benchmark run. We further argue that many benchmark reports conflate a single favorable configuration with a general performance characterization, obscuring the sensitivity of latency to these environmental factors. Drawing on the distributed systems and empirical software engineering literature on benchmarking methodology, we contend that latency, unlike throughput under saturating load, is particularly vulnerable to environmental variation because it reflects tail behavior shaped by queuing, scheduling, and coordination overheads that differ sharply across deployment contexts. We propose that reproducibility should be treated as a first-class property of latency benchmarking, requiring explicit documentation of deployment topology, resource isolation guarantees, workload generation methodology, and the statistical treatment of tail latencies, alongside artifact availability that permits independent re-execution. We conclude that without such standardization, cross-system latency comparisons drawn from published benchmarks should be interpreted with considerable caution, and that the field would benefit from community-maintained reference environments and reporting checklists analogous to those adopted in other areas of experimental computer science.
