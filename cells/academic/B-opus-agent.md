## Reproducibility Without Numbers: Latency Benchmarks as Artifacts of Deployment

Published latency figures for distributed databases are read, by practitioners choosing systems and by researchers positioning new ones, as properties of the software under test. We argue that they are more accurately understood as properties of a triple consisting of software, workload generator, and deployment substrate, and that the third term contributes enough uncontrolled variance to render the published figures unreproducible in any environment other than the one that produced them. The argument rests on the mechanics of tail latency in replicated systems: because a quorum operation completes no earlier than its slowest participant, high percentiles amplify precisely those conditions that benchmark reports conventionally leave undocumented. Among them are the placement of instances relative to the physical network topology, oversubscription and queueing behavior in the fabric, garbage collection and write amplification in the storage layer, co-tenant interference on shared hosts, and the clock discipline by which requests are timestamped. Each of these varies across providers and across regions within a single provider, and co-tenant interference varies across repetitions of the same experiment on the same account.

We then examine what the field's existing reproducibility apparatus, artifact evaluation together with the release of configuration files and load generators, does and does not secure. It secures the rerunning of a procedure; it does not secure the recovery of a result, and the distinction is easy to miss because the two coincide in single-machine benchmarking, where the substrate is inspectable and comparatively stable.

We propose that latency claims be evaluated against a weaker criterion than numerical agreement, namely the preservation of ordering among compared systems under environmental perturbation, and that reports be required to state the perturbations under which the reported ordering was observed to hold. Weakening the criterion is what makes it testable.
