## The Tyranny of the Measurable Plan

Query optimization research has spent decades perfecting an artifact that, in production, is almost always wrong. The static execution plan — chosen before a single tuple is read, from cardinality estimates that decay exponentially with join depth — remains the field's central object of study. Adaptive re-planning, the far messier discipline of noticing mid-flight that the plan is wrong and doing something about it, is treated as an engineering nicety. I want to argue that this ordering is backwards, and that the reason for it has less to do with the merits of either approach than with a methodological accident: static plan quality is trivially easy to benchmark, and adaptivity is not.

Consider what a benchmark actually rewards. Give an optimizer a fixed schema, a fixed set of statistics, and a fixed query, and you can extract a plan, cost it, execute it, and report a number. The whole apparatus is deterministic and reproducible. Two optimizers can be compared on identical inputs; a change can be attributed to a specific heuristic; a regression can be bisected. This is a genuinely excellent experimental setup, and the field has built enormous value on top of it. The trouble is that it measures the quality of a decision made under conditions that production systems rarely satisfy — statistics fresh, predicates independent, data uniform, memory uncontended, and the query one the optimizer has seen the shape of before.

Adaptivity resists this treatment at every step. Its value appears precisely where the benchmark's assumptions fail: a correlated predicate pair that collapses a join's selectivity by orders of magnitude, a data skew that arrived after the last statistics refresh, a spill that changes the effective cost of a hash build halfway through. To demonstrate an adaptive technique properly, you must construct the conditions under which the static plan goes wrong — and the moment you do, a skeptic will accuse you of building a strawman. Choose mild misestimation and your gains look marginal. Choose severe misestimation and you look like you rigged it. There is no neutral ground, because the size of the win is a function of how wrong the plan was, and how wrong the plan was is exactly the variable you stand accused of choosing.

The consequence is a literature that under-reports the value of the thing production engineers most reliably need. Ask anyone who operates a large analytical system where their pain lives, and they will not describe an optimizer that picked the second-best join order. They will describe a query that ran fine for months and then, one morning, did not — and a system that had no mechanism to notice, and no vocabulary for changing its mind.
