## What We Measure Instead

The chart is always the same chart. Latency down one axis, some workload across the other, and a line sitting a satisfying distance below whatever the previous generation managed, which is a genuinely useful thing to know and also, if it is the only thing being reported, a quiet confession about what the field has decided is worth knowing.

Static plan quality submits to measurement with almost embarrassing ease. Hand the optimizer a query, take the plan it produces, execute it, record the number, and you have a result that another group can reproduce on other hardware, that a reviewer can compare against a table of prior systems in the time it takes to finish a coffee, and that a vendor can put on a slide without anyone reasonably objecting. Adaptive re-planning offers no such convenience. Its value lands almost entirely in the tail, in the query where the cardinality estimate missed by three orders of magnitude, or where the data skewed along a dimension nobody thought to model, or where a join that costed out as trivial turned out to be the whole query, and those cases are, by construction, the ones a well-designed benchmark works hardest to exclude. A benchmark aspires to be stable and representative. Adaptivity earns its keep exactly when the world is neither.

The field therefore optimizes what it can see, which is not a conspiracy so much as a gradient that everyone involved has been walking down for twenty years while behaving, individually, quite sensibly. Researchers write toward what gets accepted, engineers build toward what gets measured, procurement teams believe what wins the bake-off, and the accumulated result is an enormous and genuinely impressive body of work on producing a better initial plan, sitting alongside a near-total absence, in most systems actually running in production, of machinery for noticing partway through that the initial plan was wrong.

It is going to be wrong. Not as an occasional embarrassment but as a structural property of the approach, because cardinality estimation is the load-bearing assumption under every cost model in every optimizer ever shipped, and cardinality estimation across multi-way joins with correlated predicates is, in the general case, a problem we have collectively agreed to stop being surprised about.

Taking the alternative seriously would mean treating the plan as a hypothesis rather than a decision, which sounds like a modest reframing and is not. It would mean instrumenting operators to report what they are seeing rather than only what they emitted, accepting that a query's shape may change halfway through execution, and then living with the consequences for memory accounting, for spill behavior, and for the comfortable fiction (load-bearing in more codebases than anyone wants to audit) that a plan is a static object with a beginning and an end. The result is harder to build, considerably harder to reason about, and much less likely to produce a clean chart, which is to say it has all the properties of a thing that gets deferred to next quarter.
