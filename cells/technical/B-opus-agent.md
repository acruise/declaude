## What the Benchmark Holds Still

Every query engine begins each execution with a guess. Before a row moves, the optimizer commits to a join order, a set of physical operators, a distribution strategy, and a memory budget, and it does so on the strength of statistics that were true at some point in the past and independence assumptions that correlated columns violate as a matter of course, all of it priced by cost constants calibrated against hardware the deployment may not resemble. None of this is controversial. Cardinality estimation error compounds with join depth, so that by the time a plan reaches its deeper joins the optimizer may be reasoning about a row count wrong by orders of magnitude, and every engine team knows it.

What ought to follow is heavy investment in the machinery that catches the wrong guess in flight: re-deriving a join order once real cardinalities arrive at a materialization point, flipping the build and probe sides of a hash join, promoting a broadcast to a shuffle when the small side turns out not to be small, injecting a semi-join filter that the plan-time statistics gave no reason to want. Several engines do some of this, usually at one or two designated points where the pipeline already breaks. Very few treat it as the spine of execution, and those that do generally got there by way of an incident review rather than a research program.

The explanation is methodological: static plan quality is cheap to measure, and what is cheap to measure ends up standing in for what is valuable. Consider what a benchmark actually holds still. It holds the schema fixed, the data distribution fixed, the statistics fresh, the query set published well in advance, and the predicate selectivities drawn from whatever distribution the generator was told to draw them from. Under those conditions the optimizer's guess is very nearly the right guess, because the conditions are precisely the ones under which plan-time information is complete. Two optimizers can then be diffed plan by plan, scored on which one picks the better join order, and ranked. The resulting number is legible, and legible numbers are what move roadmaps.

Adaptivity, measured on the same rig, looks like overhead. Its contribution is the disaster that did not happen, and it arrives on exactly the inputs a benchmark is built to exclude: the skewed key carrying most of the rows, the stale histogram after a bulk load, the parameterized query whose bound values this morning have nothing in common with yesterday's, the user-defined function the cost model holds no opinion about. Those are the ordinary conditions of production and the pathological conditions of a benchmark, which is why the engineering that addresses them scores as a rounding error, or as a regression, on the leaderboard everyone reads.
