## The On-Call Ratchet

Every on-call rotation I have inherited was in worse shape than the day it was set up, and after enough of them I stopped attributing this to the particular carelessness of the particular team and started looking at the shape of the thing itself.

Consider what it costs to add an alert. Something breaks, nobody notices for forty minutes, and the postmortem produces an Action Item, with an owner and a due date: alert on the thing that broke. The work is small, the justification is a story everyone in the room has just lived through, and the reviewer approving the change is rarely the person who will be woken by it. Now consider what it costs to remove one. You have to stand in front of your colleagues and assert a negative, that this condition will not matter the next time it fires, about a rule someone wrote in the aftermath of an outage they remember far better than you do. Those two operations are not symmetric, and any system in which additions are cheap and deletions are expensive can only travel in one direction.

Thresholds decay by a stranger mechanism, which is that they were correct. A queue-depth alarm set when the service carried a fraction of its current traffic was a good alarm on the day it was written; the service grew, the alarm did not, and it now fires most weekends during the batch window. Nobody changed anything, which is precisely the problem, since the threshold is a snapshot of a system that has since moved out from under it. Responders adapt faster than configuration does. Within a couple of months the page has been reclassified in everyone's head from "something is wrong" to "it's Saturday," and that reclassification attaches to the channel rather than to the individual rule: same pager, same sound at three in the morning, answered now by someone whose first instinct is to acknowledge before reading.

Runbooks rot for a reason worth stating carefully. A runbook records a system at the moment someone understood it, which is usually the week after an incident, and the thing most likely to generate the next page is that same system having changed. The document goes stale fastest in exactly the areas where it is consulted most, and the person best placed to notice is on hour six of an incident at an hour when nobody edits documentation.

Then the people rotate out. The engineer who could reason from first principles about why that alarm existed takes another job or a promotion, and what she leaves behind is the alarm along with the runbook that cites it, neither of which contains the model that produced them.
