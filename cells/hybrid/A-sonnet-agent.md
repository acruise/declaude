## Why On-Call Rotations Rot

The first version of any on-call rotation is a clean idea. Five engineers, one week each, a pager that only rings for things that matter. Everyone who joins the rotation understands the system, because the system is small enough to fit in a head. The runbook is short because it has to be — nobody has written the tenth edge case yet, because the tenth edge case hasn't happened yet.

Then the system grows, and the rotation doesn't grow with it, not in the way that matters. You add services. You add teams. You add a dependency on a queue that someone else owns, and a retry policy that someone else tuned, and a timeout that made sense in isolation and stopped making sense the moment three of them were stacked end to end. Each addition is reasonable on its own. Nobody sits down and decides to make the pager worse. But the pager gets worse anyway, the way a coastline erodes — not from any single storm, but from the ordinary weather of shipping software.

What actually decays isn't the infrastructure. Infrastructure, if anything, gets more resilient over time — more retries, more redundancy, more alerting. What decays is the map in the responder's head. The person who wrote the payment service left eighteen months ago. The person who inherited it has never read the code, only the alerts, and the alerts have their own folklore: this one always fires around 2 a.m. and it's fine, this one means the disk, this one nobody's sure about but you restart the pod and it goes away. That folklore is knowledge, technically, but it's knowledge with no foundation under it — a set of learned responses to symptoms, detached from causes, passed from one on-call engineer to the next like a rumor that started true.

Runbooks are supposed to stop this. In practice they're a snapshot of a system that no longer exists by the time anyone reads them under pressure. I've opened a runbook at 3 a.m. and watched it describe a dashboard that was deprecated, pointing at a rollback procedure for a deploy pipeline that had since been replaced twice. The runbook wasn't wrong when it was written. It was wrong the way a photograph is wrong — accurate to a moment, useless as a guide to the present.

So the rotation keeps turning, and each turn it asks slightly more of whoever's holding the pager, and slightly less of the documentation meant to help them. That gap is where the decay actually lives.
