## Why On-Call Rotations Decay

I have inherited four on-call rotations over the years, and in every case the rotation I inherited bore only a rough resemblance to the rotation someone had originally designed. The escalation policy still named an engineer who had left the company. The runbook linked to a wiki page that redirected to a login screen. The alert that fired most reliably at 3 a.m. was not the alert anyone had intended to be the reliable one; it was the one whose threshold had never been revisited since the service it watched was rewritten underneath it, split into four smaller services, and rewritten again, each time by someone who inherited the alert without inheriting the reasoning behind its number. None of this happened through negligence, exactly. It happened because a rotation, underneath the wiki formatting and the PagerDuty schedule, is a set of assumptions frozen at the moment someone last had the energy to think hard about it, and every day after that moment is a day in which the system the assumptions describe keeps changing while the assumptions themselves do not.

That is the mechanism worth tracing, because on-call decay gets diagnosed almost every time as a morale problem, when the failure that produces the morale problem happened earlier and looks nothing like morale: it is a maintenance failure, the ordinary kind that occurs when nobody is assigned to own a piece of infrastructure's continuing correctness. A runbook is code that nobody tests. The alert thresholds sitting on top of it are claims about normal behavior, true on the day someone set them and a little less true every day afterward, that nobody revisits once normal behavior changes, and the escalation path behind those alerts is an org chart that keeps its old shape through promotions, departures, reorgs, and the ordinary passage of time, sitting inside a system with no built-in reason to notice the mismatch until the night it matters most.

I want to be careful here not to blame the individuals who let this happen, because I have been one of them more than once. I have owned rotations I did not touch for months at a stretch, on the reasonable theory that a quiet pager is a working pager. It usually is. But quiet only measures the absence of a signal, and the absence of a signal is a poor proxy for correctness the moment the underlying system moves and the alert does not move with it. That gap, the long stretch where nothing forces anyone to look, is where a rotation spends most of its life, and it is also where it quietly stops being the thing anyone actually designed.
