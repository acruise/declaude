## Severity

The page arrived sometime in the small hours, and I know I answered it before I was awake, because the first thing in the channel under my name is a single lowercase "k", which is what my thumb produces when the rest of me is still offline. I could give you the time. I would be inventing it: I have told this story often enough that the retellings have sanded the edges off whatever I originally saw, and any timestamp I offered now would be reconstruction delivered with a confidence it has not earned.

The dashboards were green. That is the part I am certain of, because it is the part I argued about. The consumers were up and the error rate was flat, and behind both reassurances a queue was filling like a tub with the drain shut, since the health check we had written asked whether the process was running and never thought to ask whether it was doing anything.

Priya had gotten there first, and her message was still sitting alone at the top of the channel when I joined: "is this real or is it the metric." At that hour it is a reasonable question, and we had trained each other to ask it, having spent the previous winter chasing a monitoring bug that fired at roughly this time of night and meant nothing, so we gave the question a good deal of our attention while the queue went on filling.

By the time anyone opened a terminal, we had been classifying for a while. Someone pulled up the escalation policy, a wiki page nobody had edited since the Reorg and that everybody could nonetheless quote, because the quotable part was the part about sleep. A Sev-3 gets a ticket. A Sev-2 wakes the on-call manager. A Sev-1 wakes the director, who then wakes people with the kind of memory that outlasts the incident.

I want to say we were being careful. We were being careful. The care was simply pointed at the wrong object: we were not measuring the damage, we were pricing it, and the currency was other people's nights and the credibility we would spend by waking them for something that turned out, again, to be the metric.

Nobody in that channel had the authority to declare an emergency. What we had was a dropdown with four values in it and an unspoken agreement about what each one cost, and the thing we actually decided, somewhere in the third hour, was which word to put in the field.
