## Severity One

The page came in at 2:14 in the morning, which is the hour when the body has the least to offer.

I remember the sound more than the screen. Not the alert itself — everyone's alert sounds the same, some sterile marimba chosen by a committee — but the sound of my own hand finding the phone on the nightstand, knocking over a glass of water, not caring. That's the thing about being on call. You don't wake up. You get retrieved.

By 2:20 there were nine people in a video call. By 2:40 there were fourteen. Nobody had told the extra five to join. They joined because the channel had gone quiet in the particular way channels go quiet when something is actually wrong, and they wanted to see it.

Here's what I've come to believe: an organization doesn't have a definition of "emergency." It has a *behavior*, and the behavior is the definition.

We had a severity matrix. Of course we had a severity matrix. It lived in a wiki page that had been edited four times in three years, and it defined a Sev1 as a total loss of a customer-facing service, a Sev2 as a partial degradation, a Sev3 as an internal impairment with no customer visibility. Clean. Reasonable. Utterly beside the point.

What actually happened was this: someone looked at a graph, said "that's bad," and fourteen people believed them. The matrix was never consulted. It has, as far as I know, never been consulted by anyone, ever, at 2 a.m.

And that's not a failure of the matrix. It's a misunderstanding of what a matrix is for. The matrix isn't a decision procedure. It's a *record* of a decision procedure that lives somewhere else — in tenure, in social credit, in whose voice makes a room go still.

We fixed it by 5. The postmortem was thorough, blameless, and produced eleven action items, of which two were completed.

But I keep coming back to 2:40, and those five uninvited people. Nobody escalated to them. Nobody had to.

The system knew.
